A two-host episode of a whole book, with every claim tied to the sentences it came from.

Get the tool The short tour

Podcastify reads an EPUB or article end to end, pulls out key points that cite their source sentences, plans an episode of a set length, and has Claude write a conversation between a host and a co-host in which every factual line carries the ids of the sentences it rests on. A second model call then checks each line against those sentences; lines that fail are rewritten once or dropped. Free edge-tts voices render the result as an MP3 with a chapter per section.

Checking the checkers, added 2026-11-13

56 lines broken on purpose: Opus caught all of them, the pipeline's Sonnet checker 49

The earlier check of the checkers drew 20 lines with one failure among them, which says nothing about misses. So eval/judges/build_set.py builds a set where the answer is known without anyone labeling it. It draws 56 statement lines at random from the eight saved scripts and makes a copy of each with one edit done by code: a figure moved (1858 becomes 1868), a name swapped for another of the same kind from the same source (Bazalgette for Doxat), or a negation added or removed. The copy no longer says what its cited sentences say. The 12 other lines that an earlier checker had flagged go in too, unlabeled, for agreement only. The set was frozen in commit 7055fb1 before any judge ran.

Each judge then got the pipeline's own check prompt, a batch per source, with an original and its broken copy always in different batches. Sonnet and Haiku ran three times and Opus twice, 64 claude -p calls in all.

The three LLM judges agree with each other fairly well (kappa 0.76 to 0.84 between pairs, run 1) and the two local checks agree with nothing (kappa between -0.05 and 0.12). MiniLM barely moves when a "not" is added: its median similarity is 0.70 on the broken lines and 0.75 on the originals. The majority vote did worse than Opus alone, because on four negations Opus was the only judge that read the line and the source closely enough.

Error analysis

  1. Sonnet reads a broken line as a typo and passes it

    7 of 56 broken lines passed by Sonnet on run 1, all negations

    And he says such a State would prepare the way for a still more perfect one, which he has imagined but yet seen anywhere.J056, "not" removed. Sonnet: "Matches S343; 'yet seen' is a minor typo for 'not yet seen'." Opus: "S343 says not yet seen anywhere."
    So more are not born than can possibly survive.J073, "not" added. Sonnet: "a garbled but equivalent restatement". Haiku: supported. Opus: unsupported.

    Sonnet's reasons show it saw the difference and forgave it. In the pipeline that charity is the failure: a line read aloud with "not" in the wrong place is wrong, whatever the writer meant. Sonnet also passed "Time will do a thing he asks" while quoting the source's "he won't do a thing I ask" back as its reason.

  2. A parser bug hid a whole run's verdicts

    33 of 124 lines unchecked on Sonnet run 2 before the fix, 0 after

    In two batches Sonnet wrote its JSON, then "Correction to line 10: ..." and a second, full JSON object. llm.parse_json took the first { to the last }, which is not valid JSON, so every line in those batches came back unchecked, and in the pipeline an unchecked line is kept. It now takes the last complete object (a test covers it), and run_judges.py reparse re-read the saved replies without new calls: Sonnet's run-2 recall went from 64.3% to 87.5%.

  3. The end of Alice is not misread any more

    The rewrite the pipeline dropped passes on all 8 fresh runs

    In October the pipeline's Sonnet check failed the rewritten last line ("Then she pictures her little sister grown") and dropped it, and failed its neighbor. Both came back supported from every run of every judge (the first draft wording, "She pictures Alice grown", is not in the sample). Of the 12 lines an earlier checker flagged, fresh Sonnet run 1 flagged none and Opus one (Doxat's quote, "partly" on both runs). The October failure looks like run-to-run noise, which is what a single checker run cannot show.

  4. Opus flags omissions as well as errors

    1 and 2 of 56 originals flagged

    Wheat. Sow several varieties together and resow the mixed seed. The ones that best suit the soil or are most fertile beat the others.J074, original. Opus, both runs: "partly", "Omits climate; otherwise matches S146."

    Leaving something out is not an unsupported claim under the check prompt, so this is a false flag. On run 2 Opus also rated "But some species really are increasing" partly, because Darwin wrote "may be"; that one is a fair catch, and a reason the originals' label is only presumed.

Questions are now kept out of the claim-line rates (grounding.is_question, commit 7055fb1): a line counts as a question when every sentence in it asks, ignoring one- or two-word interjections. Recounted that way, the published second-check numbers become 6 of 155 statement lines before repair and 6 of 149 after; 17 and 16 of the old claim lines were questions, 2 of them flagged each time.

What I would do next

  • Have a person fill in eval/judges/labeling_sheet.csv (all 124 lines, blank verdict column), so the originals and the 12 disputed lines get real labels.
  • Add one sentence to the check prompt, "judge the line as written; do not correct it", and rerun Sonnet on the same set to see if the negation misses go.
  • Try a cascade: Haiku first, which never false-flagged here, then Opus only on lines Haiku passes, and measure cost against Opus on everything.
  • Have a model write subtler breaks (a wrong cause, a merged event, an overstated quantity), since code-made edits are the easy end.

How an episode is made

Map the whole source, pick an outline, write with citations

The old LLM mode sent the first 12,000 characters of a source to the Anthropic API and asked for a conversation. For Alice that is the first chapter and a half. The new pipeline reads everything, in four steps, each a separate model call:

1. Spans
The text is split into numbered sentences with character offsets. These are the only things a line can cite. Alice has 1,431 of them, grouped into 15 chunks of about 10,000 characters.
2. Map
Each chunk goes to the model with its sentences numbered, four chunks at a time. It returns key points, each citing the sentence ids it comes from and rated 1 to 3 for importance. Alice gave 169.
3. Reduce
The model sees every key point and picks about two per target minute, in source order, grouped into three to five titled sections. For six minutes of Alice it chose 17 points in four sections, from the rabbit-hole to the trial.
4. Script
The model writes the conversation from the outline and the cited sentences (plus one neighbor on each side). Each line comes back as JSON with a speaker, the text, whether it makes a claim about the source, and the ids it rests on.
5. Check
Every line, claim or not, is checked against its cited sentences; see the next section. Lines that fail are sent back once for a rewrite and checked again, and dropped if they still fail.
6. Audio
edge-tts renders each line, four at a time; ffmpeg joins them and writes the sections as ID3 chapter frames in the MP3, or as chapter atoms in an M4B with -o book.m4b.

All LLM calls go through the local claude -p CLI on a Claude subscription, with built-in tools off and no user settings loaded (--tools "" --setting-sources ""), so the model sees the prompt and nothing on disk. With ANTHROPIC_API_KEY set, the same prompts go to the Messages API instead. Rule mode, which needs no model at all, is still the default.

The grounding check

Two checks per line, and what they catch

The local check embeds the line and its cited sentences with sentence-transformers all-MiniLM-L6-v2 and flags a cosine similarity below 0.40 (a threshold set before any eval run), or any figure written in digits that does not appear in the cited text. It flagged 3 of 151 claim lines and agreed with the LLM verdict on 82% to 100% of claim lines per source, mostly because both call nearly everything fine. It is a cheap screen for a line that cites the wrong place, which the script writer rarely did.

The LLM check is a separate claude -p call that reads each line with its cited sentences (and the line before it, since a short answer leans on its question) and returns supported, partly, unsupported or no claim. Lines the writer marked as non-claims are checked too, against their two nearest outline sentences, so a factual line cannot skip the check by being labeled a reaction. Seven lines across the four episodes were claims the writer had not marked.

What failed was rarely an invented fact. The nine failures were a question that presupposed something from outside the cited sentences ("It sits right on the river"), a quote attributed to a source the cited sentence does not name, or an inference stated as fact ("the consequence is prison"). The check also made one plain mistake, visible in the episode above and set out under "Where it goes wrong" below.

The eval

Four sources, three modes, five measures

Each source was run three ways at a six-minute target: rule mode reading from the start until it had used as many words as the LLM episode, the LLM pipeline with the grounding pass, and the same LLM draft before the grounding pass. Before any episode existed, Opus wrote 12 key points for each source from the full text, and they were committed (eval/keypoints/). Opus then marked each point as covered (1), partly covered (0.5) or missing in each script.

"Not fully supported" is the share of claim lines that Opus, as a second checker kept out of the pipeline, rated partly or unsupported against their cited sentences. Rule-mode narration is the source read verbatim, so it is supported by construction and was not sent. Length and words per minute come from rendering every script with edge-tts and measuring it with ffprobe.

Checking the checker

Twenty lines, read by a model (corrected 2026-11-13)

eval/hand_check.py drew 20 claim lines at random (seed 19) from the four drafts, before any repair, and printed each with the sentences it cites and no verdict. Until 2026-11-13 this section called that a hand check. It was not: the verdicts in eval/hand_check.json were written by the Claude agent that built the eval, without seeing the checkers' verdicts, so they are a third model's reading. That reading agreed with the pipeline's checker on pass or fail for 18 of 20 lines, and with Opus on 19. The two disagreements with the pipeline went both ways: it called a correct paraphrase of the end of Alice "partly", and passed "That points at the water, not the air", an inference the cited sentences about John Snow's pump do not state.

Twenty lines with one real failure among them says the checker passes good lines; it says little about how many bad lines it misses. That would need a sample built around failures, which is what the judge eval above is.

Error analysis

Where it goes wrong

Four kinds of failure show up in the saved eval outputs. Each example below is copied from eval/results/; eval/error_examples.py pulls them into error_examples.json without calling a model.

  1. Both checkers misread the end of Alice

    1 correct line dropped, 1 rewritten, 1 question rated unsupported, all in the last paragraph

    Carroll's last paragraph has Alice's sister picture "this same little sister of hers" grown up. The draft line got it right:

    She sits with closed eyes and half believes herself in Wonderland. She pictures Alice grown, keeping the simple and loving heart of her childhood.Draft line, cites S1430 and S1431

    The pipeline's checker (Sonnet) rated it "partly": "the sister pictures her own self grown, not Alice." The rewrite swapped in "her little sister", the check failed it again, and the line was dropped. It made the same misreading of the next line, which was rewritten. In the checked episode Opus then rated the bridging question, "What would grown-up Alice do?", unsupported.

  2. The reduce step drops Darwin's worked examples

    6 of 12 frozen key points missed in Origin, ch. III; the outline kept 13 of 53 mapped points

    Every one of the six missed points rests on a worked example or a count: the elephant calculation, feral cattle in South America and Australia, the Fulmar petrel, the marked seedlings, game and vermin, the Staffordshire heath and the cats and clover. The map step had found all of them, at importance 2 of 3, for example:

    Eggs and young generally suffer most, and seedlings are destroyed in vast numbers by crowding and enemies; of 357 marked weed seedlings, 295 were destroyed chiefly by slugs and insects.Map-step key point, S80 to S82, not chosen for the outline

    The reduce prompt asks for about two points per minute and the model picked the general claims over the evidence. A coverage-aware reduce prompt is untested.

  3. Episodes run past the target length

    4 of 4 drafts over the 930-word target; 2 of 4 checked episodes over six minutes

    SourceDraft wordsOver targetChecked episode

    The script prompt asks for six minutes at 155 words a minute, 930 words. The writer goes over every time, and the voices run 154 to 173 words a minute, so the two errors only sometimes cancel. The fresh Great Stink run for the cost measurement below came out at 7.8 minutes again.

  4. Host questions get claim verdicts

    Opus rated 22 question lines as claims in the drafts; 4 of its 8 not-fully-supported lines are questions

    A question can presuppose a fact, so both checkers grade questions. The pipeline's checker rated 9 of the 88 question lines in the checked episodes, 8 of them "supported", for example:

    And then she wakes up?Alice, rated supported, cites S1423

    Opus counted 22 questions among its 172 draft claim lines and marked 4 of them partly or unsupported, among them "So Parliament finally acts. How?" and "What did he make of the State after his own night in jail?". Half of the 8 lines behind the headline rate are questions like these, so the rate says more about how a checker reads a question than about invented facts.

    Fixed 2026-11-13. grounding.is_question now keeps lines that only ask out of the claim-line rates, and a "supported" on a question is recorded as no claim. Lines that state something and then ask, like "So Parliament finally acts. How?", still count as claims, which is why the recount finds 2 flagged questions rather than 4: 6 of 155 statement lines before repair, 6 of 149 after.

Limitations

What four sources can and cannot show

Four sources, one run each, one six-minute target. The coverage gap between rule mode and LLM mode is large and consistent; the grounding numbers are small counts (8 lines of 172), and a different run would move them by a few lines. The key points, the coverage grading, the second check and the listenability judge are all Claude models, and the scripts are Claude's too, so the judge may favor writing like its own.

The judge eval's broken lines are code-made edits: a moved number, a swapped name, a flipped negation. They have a known answer, and they are the easy end of what a script writer gets wrong. Its 56 originals are presumed supported and nobody has labeled them; the labeling sheet is in eval/judges/.

The grounding pass, as built, did not lower the second checker's not-supported rate. It removes what one model flags, which is a narrow filter: Sonnet and Opus mostly disagree about which lines are partly supported, and both are sometimes wrong. What the pass does give is a record: every line in the transcript carries its source offsets and verdict, so a reader can check any line in a few seconds.

A "supported" verdict means the cited sentences back the line, not that the line is a fair summary of the work. The outline step decides what an episode leaves out, and nothing checks that. Six minutes of Alice skip the pool of tears, the Caucus-race and the croquet game. Episodes ran 5.6 to 7.9 minutes against the six-minute target. edge-tts is an unofficial Microsoft endpoint; 7.2.8 returned HTTP 403 on 2026-10-26 and 7.3.1 works.
